Game Theory: When Everyone Acting Smart Makes Everyone Worse Off

Two prisoners, each doing the rational thing, both end up worse than if they'd cooperated. This one puzzle explains arms races, price wars, and climate stalemates.

Builds on↩ Exponential Intuition

Here’s a puzzle that has haunted economists for 70 years. Two partners in crime are interrogated separately. If both stay silent, they get light sentences. If one betrays the other, the betrayer walks free and the silent one rots. If both betray, both get medium sentences. The gut-punch: no matter what the other does, betraying is the ‘rational’ choice — so both betray, and both end up worse than if they’d trusted each other. Welcome to game theory.

The prisoner's dilemma

Work through it from one prisoner’s view: ‘If my partner stays silent, betraying sets me free (better than a light sentence). If my partner betrays, betraying gives me medium instead of the worst. Either way, betraying wins.’ Both reason identically, both betray, both get the medium sentence — when mutual silence would have been better for both. Individually rational, collectively terrible. That gap is the whole field.

💡
This is everywhere

Arms races (both nations overspend on weapons), price wars (both stores slash prices into the ground), overfishing, doping in sports, climate inaction — all prisoner’s dilemmas. Each player, acting sensibly for themselves, produces a result nobody wanted. Spotting the shape tells you why smart people get stuck in bad outcomes: the incentives are the trap, not the people.

📜
The escape hatch: repetition

One-shot, betrayal wins. But if you’ll play many rounds with the same person, cooperation can emerge — the best-known strategy is tit-for-tat: start nice, then mirror what the other did last time. Reputation, contracts, laws, and ongoing relationships are all ways humans turn a one-shot dilemma into a repeated game where trust pays. Most of civilization is machinery for escaping the prisoner’s dilemma.

The gist

  • Game theory studies decisions where your best move depends on others’ moves.

  • In the prisoner’s dilemma, betraying is individually rational — so both betray and both lose.

  • Individually rational + collectively terrible = the core tension.

  • Arms races, price wars, overfishing, climate inaction are all this shape.

  • Repetition (and reputation, contracts, law) lets cooperation emerge — e.g. tit-for-tat.
