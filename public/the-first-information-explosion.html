The Printing Press: History's First Viral Moment

One machine dropped the cost of copying an idea by orders of magnitude — and toppled churches, sparked science, and remade Europe.

Builds on↩ Why Some Nations Are Rich

Before ~1440, a book was copied by hand — months of a monk’s labour for one copy. Then Gutenberg combined movable type with a wine press, and suddenly you could print hundreds of identical pages a day. The cost of copying an idea collapsed. What followed — the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution, mass literacy — is what happens when information suddenly gets cheap. Today: humanity’s first taste of things going viral.

Cheap copies change everything

The press didn’t just make books faster — it changed who could have ideas and how fast they spread. When copying is expensive, knowledge stays locked with a tiny elite (remember the scribe class from the writing lesson). When copying is cheap, ideas escape. A pamphlet could reach thousands in weeks; a challenge to authority could outrun any attempt to suppress it.

📜
It broke a monopoly on truth

Luther’s 95 Theses went ‘viral’ in 1517 — printed, translated, and spread across Germany in weeks, something impossible a century earlier. The Catholic Church could not un-print them. For the first time, an institution’s monopoly on which ideas circulated was broken by a machine. Every information technology since has re-run this fight between gatekeepers and cheap copying.

⚠️
Cheap ideas aren't always true ideas

The press spread science and witch-hunting manuals and propaganda. Lowering the cost of copying amplifies whatever spreads — good and bad alike. If that sounds like the internet and social media, it should: same mechanism, new century. Cheap distribution is power, not virtue.

The gist

  • The press (~1440) collapsed the cost of copying an idea.

  • Cheap copies let knowledge escape the elite and spread fast.

  • It fueled the Reformation, Scientific Revolution, and mass literacy.

  • It broke institutional monopolies on which ideas could circulate (Luther went ‘viral’).

  • Cheap distribution amplifies good and bad alike — the same dynamic as today’s internet.
