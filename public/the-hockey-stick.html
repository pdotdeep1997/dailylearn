The Hockey Stick: When Human Life Suddenly Got Better

For thousands of years, almost nothing changed. Then, around 1800, growth went vertical. Understanding that elbow is understanding the modern world.

Builds on↩ How We Learned to Learn

Plot human wellbeing — income, lifespan, energy use — across history and you get one of the most startling shapes in all of data: a flat line for millennia, then a sudden near-vertical spike around 1800. A hockey stick. For almost all of history your great-grandchildren lived like you did. Then the Industrial Revolution hit and living standards began doubling within lifetimes. Today: what bent the line, and why it matters.

Energy is the unlock

The deep story is energy. For all of history, work meant muscles — human and animal — plus a little wind and water. Then coal + the steam engine let us convert stored energy into unlimited mechanical work, on demand, anywhere. Suddenly one machine did the work of hundreds of people. Output per person exploded, and for the first time growth outran population, so average living standards could actually rise instead of being eaten by more mouths.

💡
Science met the workshop

This didn’t come from nowhere — it’s the Scientific Revolution (yesterday) cashing out. Systematic experiment produced reliable knowledge; inventors and entrepreneurs applied it to iron, steam, and textiles; and institutions that let people profit from invention (patents, markets) turned tinkering into a self-fueling engine of growth. Knowledge + energy + incentives = the hockey stick.

⚠️
The bill came too

The same engine brought brutal early factory conditions, child labour, rapid inequality, and — through fossil fuels — the carbon that drives today’s climate challenge. The Industrial Revolution is the clearest case of a change being overwhelmingly transformative and carrying costs we’re still paying. Progress and its price arrived together.

The gist

  • Human living standards were ~flat for millennia, then went vertical around 1800 — the hockey stick.

  • The unlock was energy: coal + steam turned stored energy into unlimited on-demand work.

  • Output per person outran population, so average living standards could finally rise.

  • It was the Scientific Revolution applied — knowledge + energy + incentives compounding.

  • It also brought harsh labour, inequality, and fossil-fuel carbon — progress and its price together.
