The Choice That Makes Code 1,000× Faster (or Slower)

Same computer, same data, same task — but pick the wrong way to store your stuff and it grinds to a halt. Meet Big-O.

Builds on↩ Why Your Computer Is Always Waiting

Two programs do the exact same job on the exact same laptop. One finishes instantly; the other takes an hour. The difference isn’t the CPU or the language — it’s how the data was organized and how the work scales as data grows. This is the idea that makes senior engineers wince at junior code: Big-O notation, the language of ‘how does this slow down as things get big?’ Yesterday: where data lives. Today: how your choices scale.

Big-O: how cost grows, not how long it takes

Big-O ignores the stopwatch and asks: as the input gets N times bigger, how much more work? A few flavors you’ll meet everywhere:

  • O(1) — constant. Same cost no matter how big N gets. (Grab item #5 from an array.)
  • O(log n) — halve the problem each step. Blazing even for huge N. (Binary search.)
  • O(n) — linear. Twice the data, twice the work. (Scan a list.)
  • O(n²) — quadratic. 10× the data = 100× the work. (Compare every pair — the junior-code trap.)

The magic isn’t precision; it’s seeing the shape of the growth before it bites you at scale.

This is why data structures exist

A data structure is just a way of organizing data so the operations you do most are cheap. Need instant lookup by key? A hash table gives ~O(1). Need to keep things sorted and searchable? A tree gives O(log n). Need order and fast ends? A list or queue. Choosing the right structure turns an O(n²) disaster into an O(n log n) breeze.

Hashing — turning a key into a location so you can jump straight to it — is so important it gets its own lesson later (and it’s the seed of ‘consistent hashing’, which keeps giant systems from falling over). For now: the structure you pick decides your Big-O.

The gist

  • Big-O describes how work grows with input size N, not raw time.

  • Know the ladder: O(1) < O(log n) < O(n) < O(n²) — the last one explodes.

  • O(n²) hides on small inputs and detonates at scale — the classic trap.

  • A data structure organizes data so your common operations are cheap.

  • Pick the right structure (hash table, tree, list) and you pick your Big-O.

Q1

10× more data makes an O(n²) algorithm roughly how much slower?

Quadratic: (10)² = 100×. That’s why O(n²) is fine on toy data and deadly at scale.

Q2

You need instant lookup of a value by its key. Best structure?

Hash tables jump straight to the key in ~constant time. The right structure sets your Big-O.
