What Is a Company Actually Worth?

A stock isn't a lottery ticket — it's a sliver of a real business. So its value comes from the cash that business will throw off. Here's how pros pin a number on it.

Builds on↩ The Bond Market Alarm

People treat stocks like betting chips, but a share is literally a slice of a company — a claim on its future profits. So the real question isn’t ‘will the price go up?’ but ‘what is this business worth?’ Answer that, compare it to the price, and you’re investing instead of gambling. Today: the two ways professionals value a company — and why the number is always part art.

Method 1: value the future cash (DCF)

A business is worth the cash it will generate for its owners over time — brought back to today’s dollars. Estimate future free cash flows, discount each back to the present (our Day 4 tool), and sum them. That’s the intrinsic value. It’s the ‘first principles’ way: a company is a machine for producing future cash, and it’s worth the discounted pile of that cash.

Method 2: compare to peers (multiples)

The quick, market-based way: use a multiple like the P/E ratio (price ÷ earnings). If similar companies trade at 15× earnings and this one earns $10/share, a rough value is $150. Multiples are fast and reflect what the market is actually paying — but they inherit the market’s moods (in a bubble, everything’s ‘cheap’ relative to inflated peers). DCF asks ‘what’s it worth?’; multiples ask ‘what are similar things selling for?’

⚠️
Value is an opinion with a spreadsheet

Both methods rest on guesses — future growth, the discount rate, which ‘peers’ count. Nudge an assumption and the ‘value’ swings wildly, which is exactly why two analysts value the same company at half and double. Valuation isn’t finding a true number; it’s building a defensible range and knowing which assumptions drive it. Precision here is a costume, not a fact.

💡
Price vs. value

The whole game: price is what the market charges today; value is what it’s actually worth. They wander apart constantly (fear, hype, mania). Investing is buying when value comfortably exceeds price — with a ‘margin of safety’ for how wrong your estimate might be.

The gist

  • A share is a slice of a business — its value comes from future profits, not vibes.

  • DCF: discount the company’s future cash to today = intrinsic value.

  • Multiples (like P/E): value it relative to what similar companies fetch — fast, but moody.

  • Both rely on assumptions; small changes swing the answer, so build a range, not a false point.

  • Invest when value > price, with a margin of safety for being wrong.
