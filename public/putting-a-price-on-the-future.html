How to Put a Price on the Future

A company is worth the cash it'll throw off for years. A bond is a stream of future payments. So how do you price a river of money that hasn't arrived yet?

Builds on↩ The Most Important Curve in Finance

Almost everything valuable is really a promise of future money: a rental spits out rent, a bond pays coupons, a company earns profits for decades. So the trillion-dollar question is: what is a stream of future cash worth today? Yesterday you learned to run compounding backwards. Today we turn that one move into the master tool of finance — discounted cash flow — the thing every analyst, investor, and founder secretly runs in their head.

One future payment → today's value

Recall discounting: Present = Future / (1 + r)ⁿ. At a 10% discount rate, $110 next year is worth $100 now; $121 in two years is also worth $100 now. The further away the cash, the more you shrink it — because waiting costs you (opportunity, risk, inflation).

The discount rate r is the dial. Crank it up (riskier, or higher interest rates) and future money is worth less today. That single dial is why rising interest rates hammer the price of everything from tech stocks to houses.

Many payments → discounted cash flow (DCF)

A real asset pays out over many years. So you discount each future payment back to today and add them up. That sum is the asset’s fair value right now. That’s the whole DCF method:

Value = CF₁/(1+r) + CF₂/(1+r)² + CF₃/(1+r)³ + …

Estimate the future cash flows, pick a discount rate for the risk, discount, sum. Every valuation you’ll ever see — a stock, a startup, a rental, a bond — is a dressed-up version of this.

⚠️
Garbage in, garbage out

DCF feels precise, but it’s only as good as two guesses: the future cash flows and the discount rate. Nudge the discount rate a little and the ‘value’ swings wildly — which is exactly how two smart analysts value the same company at half and double. Use DCF to understand what drives value, not to pretend you’ve found one true number.

The gist

  • Most assets are a stream of future cash — the job is pricing it today.

  • Discount each future payment: PV = Future / (1+r)ⁿ; farther out = shrunk more.

  • DCF = discount every future cash flow and sum them into one value.

  • The discount rate is the master dial — higher rates crush the value of future money.

  • DCF is only as good as its inputs; use it to see what drives value, not for false precision.

Q1

At a 10% rate, is $121 received in 2 years worth more or less than $100 today?

$121 / 1.1² = $100. Discounting future cash back is the heart of DCF.

Q2

Interest rates rise sharply. What happens to the present value of far-off future cash?

Higher r means bigger denominators — future cash is worth less today. This is why rate hikes hit long-duration assets hardest.

Q3

CALLBACK: Yesterday’s compounding vs. today’s DCF — what’s the relationship?

🔄 Callback · from Day 2 · compounding

Compounding multiplies forward by (1+r); discounting divides backward by (1+r). DCF just does that for many payments and adds them.
