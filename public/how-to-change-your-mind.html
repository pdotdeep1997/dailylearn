Bayes' Theorem: How to Change Your Mind Without Losing It

New evidence lands. How much should it move you? There's an exact answer — and it's the most useful formula you'll ever internalize.

Builds on↩ Thinking in Odds

You test positive for a scary-but-rare disease on a test that’s ‘99% accurate.’ Panic? Actually… probably not. The math says you’re likely fine — and the reason is the single most powerful thinking tool in this whole track: Bayes’ theorem. It’s the rule for updating a belief when new evidence arrives. Yesterday’s boss question hinted at it; today we make it click. Master this and you’ll stop being fooled by scary headlines and shiny data.

Start with the base rate

Bayes’ big idea: your new belief depends on what you believed before. Before any test, how common is the disease? Say 1 in 1,000. That starting number is the base rate (or ‘prior’), and ignoring it is the classic blunder.

Evidence doesn’t overwrite your prior — it nudges it. A positive test makes the disease more likely than 1-in-1,000, but if the disease was fantastically rare to begin with, ‘more likely’ can still be ‘probably not.’

Why an accurate test still lies

Picture 1,000 people. 1 actually has the disease. The test is 99% accurate, so among the 999 healthy people it wrongly flags about 10 (1% false positives). So now ~11 people test positive — but only 1 is truly sick.

Your odds of being sick given a positive test? Roughly 1 in 11 ≈ 9%. Not 99%. The rareness of the disease drowns the test’s accuracy in false alarms. That’s Bayes in one gut-punch.

💡
The one-line version

New belief ∝ (how likely the evidence is if true) × (how likely it was true to begin with). Rare things need strong evidence to become likely. It’s not that the test is bad — it’s that you must weigh it against how rare the thing was. Doctors, juries, and investors get this wrong constantly.

The gist

  • Bayes updates a belief with evidence — it nudges your prior, doesn’t replace it.

  • The base rate (how common it is to begin with) is the piece people forget.

  • For rare things, even an accurate test yields mostly false positives.

  • Belief ∝ likelihood of the evidence × prior probability.

  • Rare claims demand strong evidence before you should believe them.
