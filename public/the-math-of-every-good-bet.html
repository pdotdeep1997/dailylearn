Expected Value: The Math Behind Every Good Decision

Should you take the bet, buy the insurance, launch the product? One little calculation cuts through the fog — and pros run it constantly.

Builds on↩ Thinking in Odds

Life is a series of bets under uncertainty — jobs, launches, investments, whether to bring an umbrella. Amateurs decide by gut or by what feels likely. Pros run a tiny calculation that folds together how likely each outcome is and how much it’s worth: expected value. It’s probability’s most practical child, and once it’s in your bones you’ll never look at a decision the same way. (Yes, it uses the odds you learned on Day 1.)

The formula: probability × payoff, summed

Expected value (EV) = for every possible outcome, multiply its probability by its payoff, then add them all up. It’s the average result if you could run the decision many times.

Example: a game costs $10 to play. You win $100 with probability 5%, otherwise nothing. - Win: 0.05 × $100 = $5 - Lose: 0.95 × $0 = $0 - EV of the prize = $5. But it costs $10 to play → net EV = –$5.

So on average you lose $5 every time. Fun once; ruinous forever. That’s the lens: compare the EV to the cost.

💡
Why insurance and startups both make sense

EV explains opposite-looking choices. Insurance has negative EV (the insurer profits on average) — yet it’s smart, because a rare catastrophe would wipe you out and you can’t ‘replay’ your life. Startups often have low odds but huge payoffs, so the EV can be positive even when most fail. The trick: weigh not just the average, but whether you can survive the bad outcomes long enough for the averages to pay off.

The gist

  • EV = Σ (probability × payoff) — the average outcome over many tries.

  • Decide by comparing an option’s EV to its cost.

  • Positive EV ≠ always take it; negative EV ≠ always skip it — survival matters.

  • Insurance = negative EV but protects against ruin; startups = low odds, huge payoff.

  • It’s Day 1’s probabilities, now weighted by how much each outcome is worth.

Q1

A ticket: 10% chance to win $50, else $0. EV of the prize?

0.10 × $50 = $5. If the ticket costs more than $5, it’s a losing bet on average.

Q2

Insurance usually has negative EV for you. Why can buying it still be wise?

EV is the long-run average, but survival matters. Insurance trades a small sure loss to avoid a fatal one.
