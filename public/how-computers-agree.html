How a Group of Computers Agrees on Anything

Machines crash, messages get lost, networks split. So how do a dozen servers agree on a single answer — like who owns which seat? Meet consensus.

Builds on↩ The CAP Theorem

Yesterday’s CAP theorem said that when the network splits, staying correct means the machines must agree. But agreement is shockingly hard when any machine can crash mid-sentence and messages can vanish. Yet your bank balance, that concert ticket you bought, and cloud databases all depend on many machines agreeing on one story. The trick is a consensus algorithm — and the most popular one, Raft, is built to be understandable. Today: how computers vote.

Elect a leader, replicate the log

Raft’s core idea: don’t let everyone decide — elect one leader. The machines hold an election; whoever gets a majority of votes becomes leader for a term. All changes go through the leader, which writes them to a log and copies that log to the followers. A change is only ‘committed’ once a majority have stored it. One boss, one ordered list of changes, replicated — that’s how they stay in sync.

Why 'majority' is the magic word

Requiring a majority (a quorum) to agree is what makes it survive failures. With 5 machines, any decision needs 3. If 2 crash, the 3 survivors still form a majority and carry on. And because any two majorities must overlap in at least one machine, you can never accidentally elect two leaders with conflicting histories — the overlap machine won’t vote for a candidate that’s behind. Majorities are how the system tolerates crashes without ever contradicting itself.

💡
This is what powers 'the cloud'

Consensus (Raft, and its famously-hard predecessor Paxos) is the quiet backbone of distributed databases, coordination services (etcd, ZooKeeper), and anything that must stay consistent across machines — including the systems that hold your money and your data. When people say a database is ‘strongly consistent and fault-tolerant’, a consensus algorithm is doing the heavy lifting underneath.

The gist

  • Consensus = getting many unreliable machines to agree on one ordered story.

  • Raft elects a leader; all changes flow through it and replicate to followers as a log.

  • A change commits only once a majority (quorum) has stored it.

  • Majorities overlap, so you can’t get two conflicting leaders — crash-tolerant and consistent.

  • Consensus is the backbone of strongly-consistent, fault-tolerant distributed systems (and the cloud).

Q1

How does Raft avoid chaos among many machines?

One leader + one replicated log = a single agreed order of changes.

Q2

Why require a majority to commit a change?

Any two majorities share a machine, so conflicting histories can’t both win — and you survive a minority crashing.

Q3

CALLBACK: How does consensus relate to yesterday’s CAP theorem?

🔄 Callback · from Day 10 · CAP theorem

When the network splits, a consensus system without a majority stops committing — choosing consistency over availability. It’s CAP’s C-vs-A trade-off made concrete.
