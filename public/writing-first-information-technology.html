Writing: The First Information Technology

The earliest writing wasn't poetry or history — it was receipts. And that unglamorous origin tells you exactly why writing changed the world.

Builds on↩ The farming revolution

We imagine writing began with someone recording a great story or a law. It didn’t. The world’s earliest writing is mostly accounting — lists of grain, sheep, and taxes owed. That’s a letdown until you realize why: once farming produced surplus (our last History lesson), someone had to track who stored what, who owed whom, and what the temple collected. Writing was invented to manage information that had outgrown human memory. It was civilization’s first information technology — and every later leap, up to the internet, rhymes with it.

From tokens to tablets

In ancient Mesopotamia, traders first tracked goods with small clay tokens — one shape per commodity. As trade grew, they pressed the tokens’ marks into clay tablets instead, then simplified those marks into symbols. By around 3400–3000 BCE this became cuneiform: wedge-shaped signs pressed into clay. Egypt developed hieroglyphs, China its own script, Mesoamerica another — again, independent invention, echoing the pattern we saw with farming.

Crucially, early writing recorded quantities and things, not speech. Only later did symbols come to stand for sounds, at which point writing could capture anything language could — names, laws, stories, ideas.

💡
Why it was revolutionary

Writing let information escape the limits of a single human mind. It could be stored longer than memory, copied and moved across distance, and accumulated across generations. Knowledge stopped dying with the knower. That’s the deep function of every information technology since — the printing press, the telegraph, the computer, the internet — all doing the same three things (store, transmit, accumulate) at ever-greater scale.

📜
It also created power

Whoever could write could administer: levy taxes, enforce contracts, issue laws, and run a bureaucracy larger than any chief could hold in his head. Writing and the state grew up together — which is why literacy was long guarded by a small scribe class. Control of information has meant power from the very first tablet, a thread we’ll follow through the printing press and beyond.

The gist

  • The earliest writing was accounting — records of grain, livestock, and taxes, not literature.

  • It arose to manage surplus-driven information that had outgrown human memory (builds on the farming lesson).

  • It evolved tokens → marks → cuneiform, and was independently invented in several civilizations.

  • Only later did symbols represent sounds, letting writing capture anything language could.

  • Writing lets knowledge be stored, copied, and accumulated — the same job every later info technology does — and it concentrated power in those who controlled it.
