The Recession Alarm Hidden in the Bond Market

Bonds are just loans you can trade. Line up their interest rates and you get a curve that has predicted almost every US recession for 50 years.

Builds on↩ Who Decides the Price

Stocks get the headlines, but the bond market is bigger and, arguably, smarter. A bond is nothing exotic — it’s a loan you can buy and sell. Line up the interest rates on government bonds of different lengths and you get the yield curve, and when it does something weird — bends downward — economists brace for a recession. It’s flagged nearly every US downturn since the 1960s. Today: how a boring loan chart became Wall Street’s favorite alarm.

A bond is a tradable loan

Buy a government bond and you’ve lent the government money; it pays you interest (the yield) and returns your principal at the end. Because bonds trade, their price moves — and price and yield seesaw: when a bond’s price rises, its yield falls, and vice versa. (Why: the future payments are fixed, so paying more for them means earning a smaller return — pure discounting from our earlier lesson.)

The curve, and why it inverts

Plot yield against how long until the bond matures — 3 months, 2 years, 10 years — and you get the yield curve. Normally it slopes up: lock your money away longer, get paid more. But sometimes short-term yields rise above long-term ones — the curve inverts. That happens when the central bank holds short rates high to fight inflation, while investors, expecting a slowdown (and future rate cuts), pile into long bonds, pushing long yields down. High short + low long = the market betting trouble is coming.

⚠️
A smoke alarm, not a crystal ball

The track record is uncanny but not magic: the lag from inversion to recession runs 6–24 months (useless for timing), there’ve been false-ish alarms, and modern distortions (central-bank bond buying) can bend the curve for non-recession reasons. Treat it as one strong signal among many — impressive, not infallible.

The gist

  • A bond is a tradable loan; its price and yield move in opposite directions.

  • The yield curve plots yield vs. time-to-maturity; normally it slopes up.

  • Inversion (short yields above long) comes from high short rates + investors bracing for a slowdown.

  • It has preceded almost every US recession since the ’60s — with a 6–24 month lag.

  • It’s a strong signal, not a crystal ball — false alarms and distortions exist.
