Ranked-Choice Voting: The Math That Kills the Spoiler Effect

How letting voters rank candidates instead of picking one changes who wins — and the trade-offs no voting system can escape.

In a normal election you pick one candidate. Simple — but it has a famous flaw: a third candidate you love can ‘split the vote’ and hand victory to the one you like least. This is the spoiler effect, and it quietly shapes how people vote (and which candidates even run). Ranked-choice voting tries to fix it by asking a different question: not “who’s your favorite?” but “rank them in order.” That small change has surprisingly deep consequences.

The problem with picking just one

Under first-past-the-post (pick one, most votes wins), imagine 45% back candidate A, while 55% lean progressive but split 30/25 between B and C. The majority prefers either B or C over A — yet A wins with only 45%, because the 55% divided itself. Voters learn this and start voting strategically, abandoning their true favorite for the ‘lesser evil’ who can actually win. That pressure is exactly what ranked-choice is designed to relieve.

How the count works (instant-runoff)

  1. 1

    Voters rank candidates: 1st choice, 2nd choice, 3rd, and so on.

  2. 2

    Count everyone’s 1st choices. If a candidate has a majority (>50%), they win — done.

  3. 3

    If not, eliminate the last-place candidate and look at those ballots’ next choice.

  4. 4

    Redistribute those votes to the surviving candidates and re-count.

  5. 5

    Repeat — eliminating and redistributing — until someone crosses 50%.

💡
What it changes

Because a candidate needs broad support to reach 50%, they benefit from being lots of voters’ second choice, not just firing up a narrow base. In theory this rewards coalition-building and lets you rank your true favorite first without ‘wasting’ your vote — the spoiler pressure eases. Cities like San Francisco and states like Maine and Alaska now use it for real elections.

The honest trade-offs

Ranked-choice is not a free lunch, and supporters and critics disagree sharply. Critics point to non-monotonicity — in rare cases, ranking a candidate higher can actually cause them to lose — and to real-world confusion, longer ballot-counting, and higher rates of spoiled ballots. Supporters counter that it elects more broadly-acceptable winners and reduces negative campaigning, and that the edge cases are rare in practice.

Underneath it all sits a deeper result: Arrow’s Impossibility Theorem proved that no ranked voting system can satisfy every reasonable fairness criterion at once. So the real debate isn’t ‘which system is perfect’ but ‘which set of trade-offs do we prefer’ — a genuinely open question that reasonable people land on differently.

The gist

  • First-past-the-post suffers the spoiler effect: splitting votes can elect the least-preferred candidate.

  • Ranked-choice (instant-runoff) has voters rank candidates instead of picking one.

  • Counting eliminates the last-place candidate and redistributes their ballots until someone passes 50%.

  • It can ease spoiler pressure and reward broad appeal; used in Maine, Alaska, and cities like SF.

  • Trade-offs are real (non-monotonicity, complexity) — and Arrow’s theorem says no system is flawless.
